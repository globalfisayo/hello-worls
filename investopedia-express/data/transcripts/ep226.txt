The Efficient Market Hypothesis. Oh yeah, the holy grail of passive investing.
And according to the world's greatest investing education website, the Efficient Market Hypothesis
or theory states that share prices reflect all the information available to investors.
According to the hypothesis, stocks trade at their fair market value on exchanges while
they may spike or dip, they eventually revert to the mean, and ultimately trade based on
their real value.
That's the theory that has powered passive investing through index funds and index
ETFs for decades.
But its opponents, and they've been growing in numbers and volume for the past few years,
and believe that you can beat the market by betting on stocks like Nvidia that can deviate
way beyond their fair market values.
The Efficient Market Hypothesis was developed by Dr. Eugene Fama, an American economist,
in the 1960s and was published in his groundbreaking 1970 paper Efficient Capital Markets, a review
of theory and empirical work.
Fama argued that financial markets are informationally efficient.
This means that asset prices fully reflect all the available information out there, making
impossible to consistently achieve higher returns than the market through stock picking or market
timing. Fama actually received the Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences in 2013 for his work in
understanding asset prices. But now, a new academic paper set to be published in the American Economic
Review by researchers at the UCLA Anderson School of Management, the Stockholm School of Economics,
and the University of Minnesota Carlson School of Management say that passive investing may have
broken the efficient market hypothesis itself. They argue and show that the relentless boom in
benchmark tracking index funds and ETFs, which prompts portfolio managers to rush into the same
trades, means that the impact of each trade on prices gets amplified. Just look at the stock
market manias like the PC boom of the 1980s, the internet bubble of the 1990s, meme stocks in 2021,
and the AI revolution of the past two years. Passive fund managers have to be in those trades,
which causes over-concentration of just a handful of stocks propelling the gains and driving the
losses. Exhibit A was last Monday when investors fell out of love with Nvidia,
sending what was everyone's favorite stock down 17%, zapping $600 billion of market cap off the
company in one day. The biggest one-day dollar decline for a stock in history. There's nothing
efficient about that. And with the speed at which change and innovation is happening today,
information is at hyper speed and investors are always playing catch-up.

It's terminology time, time for us to get smart with the investing and finance term
we need to know this week. And we're going back to the fundamentals this week given that
it's earnings season. We're putting on our masks and donning our capes because the term
of the week is the CAPE ratio. According to my favorite website, the CAPE ratio, which
stands for cyclically adjusted price to earnings ratio, is used to analyze a publicly held company's
long-term financial performance while considering the impact of different economic cycles on
its earnings. The CAPE ratio, which is sometimes referred to as the Schiller CAPE ratio, since
Yale professor Robert Schiller popularized it at the height of the dot-com bubble in 1998,
considers the impact of economic influences by comparing a stock price with average earnings
adjusted for inflation over a 10-year period. You can also look at the K-B ratio over shorter
time horizons as well, like five years. The equation is simple, especially for our majors
like me. It's the company or the market's share price divided by adjusted earnings over
a five or 10-year average. The higher the ratio, the more overvalued the market, and
vice versa. And while earnings were strong last year and last quarter, earnings estimates
have been falling for this quarter and for the rest of the year. Those lower earnings
estimates are now commanding lower price to earnings multiples. Translation, valuations
have been drifting lower, which makes the stock market look less risky than so-called
risk-free assets like US Treasuries. Now, the five-year cape ratio for the S&P 500
is around 28 times earnings, down from a high of nearly 33 just a few months ago.

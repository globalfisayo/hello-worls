According to the world's greatest investing and finance website, the carried interest
This loophole allows private equity, hedge fund and venture capital managers to pay lower
tax rates on a significant portion of their income by classifying it as long-term capital
gains instead of ordinary income.
Here's how it works.
Fund managers receive a share, typically 20% of the profits generated by the investment
fund they manage.
This share is known as carried interest.
You may have heard this expression, 2 and 20 in the hedge fund world.
The 2 is the management fee and the 20 is the percent of the profits they expect
to receive and keep.
Instead of being taxed as ordinary income, which would be up to 37%, this 20% income
is taxed at a long-term capital gains rate, which is typically around 20%, a much lower
rate.
The rationale behind the loophole is that carried interest is a share of investment gains
rather than a salary, even though fund managers do not invest their own money in most cases.
So closing the carried interest loophole would mean taxing carried interest as ordinary income
rather than capital gains, ensuring that fund managers pay the same tax rates as regular
wage earners.
Kind of makes sense.

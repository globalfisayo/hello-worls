And this week, grab your trunks and a mask because we are diving into dark pools. Those
alternative trading platforms are off-exchange asset trading networks that most of us don't
have access to. According to the world's greatest investing education website, dark
pools are private asset exchanges designed to provide additional liquidity and anonymity
for trading large blocks of securities away from the public eye. And according to a
research report from Jefferies Bank and Bloomberg Data, for the first time on record, the majority,
or 51.8% of all trading in US stocks, is now consistently occurring in these dark pools,
not on public exchanges like the New York Stock Exchange or the NASDAQ. Off-exchange, or dark
pool trading, is not new to Wall Street. The dark pool trend emerged in the 1980s when the
Securities and Exchange Commission allowed brokers to transact large blocks of shares off traditional
exchanges so they wouldn't upset pricing on the major markets. Electronic trading and an SEC
ruling in 2005 that was designed to increase competition and cut transaction costs, ramped
up the number of dark pools which charge lower fees and exchanges because they're often housed
within a large investment or brokerage firm.
They don't have the overhead that the world's largest exchanges do and they can be very
selective about who gets to use the pool.
The problem for individual investors like us is that there's no pricing transparency in
dark pools yet some of the same securities we buy and sell every day are also traded
